What AI does to the psychology of learning

Most conversations about AI and education focus on tools and tasks. The deeper question is about psychological processes: what happens to metacognition, motivation, self-efficacy, and emotion regulation when AI becomes part of how students learn? The research is young and often contradictory, but the construct-level picture is already more nuanced than either AI optimism or AI panic suggests.

This page goes through each major construct from self-regulated learning theory and asks: what does the evidence actually show?

Why construct-level analysis matters

Tool-level evaluations miss the mechanism

Studies that ask "does AI improve learning outcomes?" typically measure performance on assessments. That tells you whether students got the right answers. It does not tell you whether they built the cognitive and motivational infrastructure to keep getting right answers when the AI is no longer available.

Educational psychology focuses on the underlying constructs: the metacognitive skills, motivational orientations, and self-regulatory processes that determine long-term learning capacity. AI's effect on those constructs is a separate question from its effect on immediate performance, and it is the more consequential one.

The proximal versus distal performance distinction

AI can improve proximal performance (scores on the task at hand) while degrading distal performance (ability to perform without AI in novel contexts). This distinction is well-established in educational psychology: scaffolding that is never faded can produce permanent dependency. The central empirical question for AI in education is not "does it help?" but "does the help transfer?"

Current research cannot yet answer the transfer question cleanly because most studies are short-term. But the construct-level signals we do have suggest the risk is real, particularly for metacognition and intrinsic motivation.

Construct 01

Metacognition

Metacognition -- the capacity to plan, monitor, and evaluate one's own thinking -- is the construct most directly threatened by AI offloading. Planning and monitoring require effort precisely because they are self-generated. When an AI takes over those functions, students may be bypassing the process by which metacognitive skill is built.

🧠

Metacognitive monitoring

The ability to track one's own comprehension in real time

Metacognitive monitoring develops through the experience of being wrong. Students build calibration accuracy -- the alignment between confidence and actual performance -- by repeatedly estimating, attempting, and checking. AI that provides immediate correct answers short-circuits this cycle.

Studies on LLM-assisted studying show mixed results on calibration. Several find that students who use AI explanations report high comprehension confidence but perform no better on delayed tests, a pattern consistent with reduced metacognitive accuracy rather than genuine learning.

Concern

Students using AI explanation tools show inflated confidence-accuracy correlations compared to retrieval practice conditions. The "feeling of knowing" is unreliable when content was AI-generated rather than self-constructed.

Open question

Does this effect persist over time, or do students recalibrate? No study has tracked metacognitive accuracy longitudinally across a full academic year with AI tool use as a variable.

Evidence strength
Emerging / mixed
📋

Metacognitive planning and strategy selection

Deciding how to approach a learning task before beginning

Planning is effortful because it requires the learner to represent the task, their current knowledge state, and the gap between them. AI tutors that prompt metacognitive planning -- asking students "what do you already know?" and "what's your approach?" before providing help -- consistently outperform those that simply answer questions, across multiple randomized controlled trials.

This is one of the clearest positive findings in EdAI: when AI is designed to scaffold metacognitive planning rather than replace it, measurable gains in strategic behavior follow. The design implication is direct: AI that asks before it answers supports metacognition; AI that answers without asking may erode it.

Positive signal

ITS systems with metacognitive prompting (Socratic questioning, planning scaffolds) produce measurable gains in self-reported and behavioral planning across multiple RCTs.

Concern

Students who use general-purpose LLMs without structured metacognitive prompts show no improvement in planning behavior, and some studies find deterioration on unsupported tasks.

Evidence strength
Moderately strong (ITS context)

Construct 02

Motivation

Self-determination theory distinguishes intrinsic motivation (driven by genuine interest and satisfaction) from extrinsic motivation (driven by external rewards or pressures) and from amotivation (absence of motivation). The quality of motivation, not just its quantity, predicts deep learning and long-term persistence.

AI's effects on motivation are theoretically complex: it could support intrinsic motivation by reducing frustration and enabling flow, or undermine it by removing the challenge and sense of competence that are core to intrinsic motivation. Both happen, in different students and different contexts.

🔥

Intrinsic motivation and autonomy

Learning for its own sake, driven by interest and self-determination

SDT holds that intrinsic motivation requires three basic psychological needs: autonomy (feeling of volition), competence (sense of effectiveness), and relatedness (feeling of connection). AI can satisfy or frustrate each.

Adaptive learning systems that give students meaningful control over their learning path (pacing, topic selection, difficulty) support autonomy and have consistently positive motivational effects. AI that makes decisions for students, adjusting difficulty silently without transparency, tends to reduce perceived autonomy and suppress intrinsic motivation in students who were already intrinsically motivated. The paradox: optimization designed to help can undermine motivation in the learners who need it least.

Positive signal

Transparent adaptive systems where students understand and can influence the algorithm show gains in autonomous motivation. Control matters as much as optimization.

Concern

High prior intrinsic motivation is actually a risk factor for motivational erosion under heavy AI use. Students who were self-driven can become tool-dependent without noticing the shift.

Evidence strength
Moderate, context-dependent
🎯

Goal orientation

Mastery goals (learning for growth) versus performance goals (learning for grades)

Achievement goal theory predicts that mastery-oriented students (focused on genuine understanding) will respond differently to AI than performance-oriented students (focused on grades and outcomes). Early evidence supports this: performance-oriented students are more likely to use AI to complete tasks, while mastery-oriented students are more likely to use AI as a thinking partner.

This suggests that AI amplifies pre-existing goal orientations rather than creating new ones. Institutions that emphasize performance metrics (grades, rankings) may be structurally biased toward producing the kind of AI use that erodes learning. The goal orientation a student brings to AI use determines, in large part, what they get from it.

Mixed signal

AI use predicts task completion across all goal orientations. But mastery-oriented students show better transfer to unaided tasks; performance-oriented students show minimal transfer gains.

Design implication

AI interfaces that make mastery goals salient (framing success as understanding, not answer-getting) may shift goal orientation during use. This is an active research area.

Evidence strength
Early / theoretical

Construct 03

Self-efficacy

Bandura's social cognitive theory defines self-efficacy as a person's belief in their capacity to execute the behaviors required for specific outcomes. It is domain-specific, constructed through mastery experiences, and highly predictive of persistence and effort. For AI research, the central question is whether AI assistance inflates perceived self-efficacy beyond actual capability -- and what happens when that gap is exposed.

💪

Mastery experiences and capability beliefs

Self-efficacy built through genuine success at difficult tasks

Mastery experiences -- succeeding at something genuinely challenging -- are the primary source of self-efficacy. AI-assisted success is an ambiguous mastery experience: the difficulty was real, but the agency was shared. How students attribute AI-assisted success matters enormously for what self-efficacy beliefs it builds.

Students who attribute AI-assisted success to themselves ("I solved it with a useful tool") show self-efficacy gains comparable to unassisted success. Students who attribute it to the AI ("the AI did it for me") show no self-efficacy gains. This attribution difference predicts transfer performance better than any feature of the AI tool itself.

Positive signal

AI tutors that attribute student success to the student's thinking ("you identified the key issue") produce stronger self-efficacy gains than those that attribute it to the system.

Concern

Inflated self-efficacy from AI-assisted performance can create a "confidence cliff" -- high reported self-efficacy followed by significant underperformance when AI is removed.

Evidence strength
Moderate-strong (attribution studies)

Construct 04

Emotion regulation

Learning is emotionally demanding. Frustration, anxiety, boredom, and shame are all regular parts of academic experience. Emotion regulation -- the ability to manage those states in ways that support continued engagement -- is a core component of self-regulated learning and a strong predictor of academic outcomes independent of ability.

AI has a complex relationship with academic emotion. It can reduce frustration by providing help when students are stuck. It can also remove the frustration that would have been productive. And for students with high test anxiety, AI availability changes the emotional landscape of high-stakes performance in ways the research is only beginning to map.

🌊

Test anxiety and AI availability

How the prospect of AI assistance changes anxiety regulation strategies

For students with high test anxiety, AI availability during formative practice can serve as a genuine anxiety-reduction mechanism, allowing repeated low-stakes attempts and reducing catastrophic thinking about errors. Several studies show significant anxiety reduction effects for ITS-supported practice in mathematics, where immediate corrective feedback interrupts the rumination cycle that amplifies test anxiety.

The concern is downstream: students who regulate anxiety by relying on AI availability may not develop robust emotion regulation strategies for contexts where AI is unavailable. If high-stakes assessments remain AI-free (as most do), students who learned to regulate anxiety via AI may be less prepared to regulate it through self-generated strategies when it matters most.

Positive signal

ITS with immediate corrective feedback reduces test anxiety and improves performance on low-stakes formative tasks. Effect sizes are moderate to large (d = 0.4-0.8) in well-designed studies.

Concern

Anxiety-reduction via AI availability may not transfer to unaided high-stakes settings. Students may become reliant on external regulation rather than developing internal strategies.

Evidence strength
Moderate, formative contexts only

The broader research field

Where EdAI psychology research is going

The construct-level research described above draws from several distinct research traditions that have only recently begun to converge around AI as a common subject. Understanding which tradition a study comes from helps interpret its findings and limitations.

ITS literature

Intelligent tutoring systems

30 years of controlled research, primarily in mathematics and language learning. Strong internal validity, limited generalizability to LLM-era tools. The gold standard for evidence in EdAI.

LLM studies (2022+)

Large language model research

Growing fast but methodologically heterogeneous. Short study durations, convenience samples, self-report measures. Findings should be treated as directional rather than definitive.

Learning analytics

Behavioral trace data

Large-scale VLE clickstream studies (including the KU Leuven data used in this site) provide behavioral correlates of SRL constructs. Less reliant on self-report but require careful interpretation.

SDT research

Motivation theory studies

Applies Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory to AI contexts. Theoretically grounded and increasingly empirical, but most studies measure motivation as an outcome rather than a mechanism.

Metacognition research

Monitoring and calibration studies

Uses judgment-of-learning paradigms to measure metacognitive accuracy. Increasingly applied to AI tool use, but transfer measures are rarely included. Duration is a key limitation.

Cross-cultural

Comparative and PISA studies

Most EdAI research is WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic). PISA data offers cross-cultural construct measurement but not direct AI effect measurement.

What the field still needs

The unanswered questions

RQ1

Longitudinal calibration studies

Do students who use AI extensively over one or more years show measurable degradation in metacognitive calibration accuracy compared to matched controls? No study has answered this with adequate duration and measurement rigor.

RQ2

Transfer measurement

How much of AI-assisted performance transfers to unaided performance in novel contexts? Transfer is the gold standard of learning research, but almost no AI studies measure it at adequate delays (weeks to months after treatment).

RQ3

Differential effects by SRL profile

High SRL and low SRL students likely respond very differently to the same AI tool. Studies that aggregate across learner profiles may be masking effects that are strong and directionally opposite in different subgroups.

RQ4

Design features that preserve agency

Which specific interface and interaction design choices maintain or strengthen psychological ownership of learning? This is a design science question as much as a psychology question, and the two fields have barely begun to collaborate on it.
