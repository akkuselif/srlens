AI in education: what actually works and what does not

AI tools are spreading faster in education than our understanding of their effects. The research on AI and self-regulated learning is young but already revealing clear patterns about when AI supports learning and when it undermines it.

EdAI research ITS systems LLMs in education SRL implications

The evidence so far

What works and what does not

Two decades of research on intelligent tutoring systems and the emerging research on large language models in education tells a nuanced story. AI can genuinely support learning in specific, well-designed contexts. It can also actively harm self-regulation when it removes the productive struggle that builds metacognitive skill.

Evidence supports

What works

Immediate, specific feedback on errors during practice
Adaptive difficulty that keeps students in the zone of proximal development
Prompting metacognitive reflection after tasks
Spaced repetition systems for vocabulary and factual knowledge
Natural language explanations of why an answer is wrong

Evidence raises concerns

What does not

AI that completes tasks for students rather than scaffolding them
Chatbots that answer questions without prompting the student to think first
Replacing writing practice with AI-generated drafts
Automated grading without explanation or human review
Systems that remove struggle entirely in the name of efficiency

Types of AI in education

Not all AI tools are the same

🧩

Intelligent tutoring systems

Rule-based adaptive systems like Carnegie Learning have 30 years of research behind them. Strong evidence for mathematics and language learning when used for practice, not instruction.

Strong evidence base
💬

Large language models

GPT-class models used as tutors or writing aids. Highly flexible but research is early. Key concern: students who use LLMs for answers rather than scaffolding show weaker metacognitive development.

Mixed early evidence
📊

Learning analytics

AI systems that monitor student behavior and flag at-risk learners for intervention. Most promising when insights go to educators, not just automated systems, and when they include psychological context.

Promising with caveats
✍️

AI writing tools

Tools that generate, edit, or evaluate writing. The concern for SRL is acute: writing is one of the primary contexts where students develop planning, monitoring, and revision skills.

Significant SRL risk
🎮

Adaptive learning platforms

Platforms like Duolingo that personalize content sequence and difficulty. Effective for skill building but rarely address the motivational and metacognitive dimensions of learning explicitly.

Skill-focused only
🔍

AI assessment tools

Automated essay scoring and formative assessment. Useful for rapid feedback at scale but current models struggle with higher-order thinking and can reinforce surface-level writing strategies.

Use with human review

The central problem

The bypass problem

The most significant risk of AI in education is not that it gives wrong answers. It is that it removes the productive cognitive struggle that builds self-regulation. Learning requires effort. Metacognition develops through repeated cycles of planning, monitoring failure, and adjusting. An AI that removes that cycle, however helpfully intended, removes the conditions under which self-regulated learning develops.

The design question for every educational AI tool is not "does it help students get the right answer?" but "does it build or erode the student's capacity to regulate their own learning over time?" These are very different questions with very different answers depending on how the tool is designed.

Open research questions

What we still do not know

RQ 01

Does using LLMs as study aids increase or decrease metacognitive accuracy over time? No longitudinal study has answered this cleanly yet.

RQ 02

Can AI systems detect motivational states from behavioral signals accurately enough to trigger useful interventions? Current models have high false-positive rates.

RQ 03

How do cultural differences in self-regulation interact with AI tool design? Most EdAI research is conducted in Western, English-speaking contexts.

RQ 04

What design features of AI tutors specifically support the forethought and self-reflection phases of Zimmerman's SRL cycle, not just the performance phase?
